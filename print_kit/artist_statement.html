Artist Statement.

Bloom · MFA Design + Technology · Parsons · 2026

I made Bloom because I wanted to know what was left of me when nothing was being sold.

The work started as a question I asked twenty people: when have you felt the spirit? The answers — at a concert, holding my grandmother's hand, looking up at stars in Idaho, when I make something, in the silence after a song ends — became the seed of every scene in the piece.

The installation is built around a single chair facing a 75-inch screen. A BrainBit EEG senses the visitor's stillness. A Microsoft Kinect notices their body. The screen unfolds fifteen procedurally-generated scenes — nebulae, mandalas, sand, lightning, breath — tuned in real time to the visitor's nervous system. The longer the visitor stays still, the wider the breath ring grows. The longer they lean, the deeper the bloom opens.

But the piece opens with something else. A 40-second overstimulation collage — Times Square walkers, news, ads, AI weirdness, surveillance, a wellness commercial — ending with the popup "You" Are Not Enough · Install. The visitor watches Install get pressed. What they receive is Bloom — a wellness app that holds them.

This double frame is the work's argument. Most things sold to us as presence are not present and are not for us. Wellness apps narrate at us, charge us for stillness, turn breath into a streak. Bloom is both a critique of that economy and an honest attempt to build the thing it pretends to be: a room that holds a body for six minutes while it decides what to put down.

What is being sold to you as presence,
and what is presence when nothing is being sold?

The third element — a cork wall with three engraved prompts — asks the visitor to leave one card. Something beautiful. Their happy place or person. Something they fear. By Saturday night the wall is the show's analog memory: strangers' handwriting, accumulated, anonymous, unsponsored.

For the visitors who want to stay, a small altar at the back of the room holds a glass cube on a slow turntable, flanked by two mirrors creating an infinity tunnel. An Apple Vision Pro offers a ninety-second image-tracked coda — a quieter second beat after the chair.

The work is finally a small argument for staying. I built it because I wanted to make something that listened more than it spoke. Because the meditative industrial complex has gotten very loud. Because I needed to know whether I could make a room that, for six minutes, did not ask anything of the person sitting in it.

You have always been bloom.

Siddharth Mehta
